Waste needs a route back into production.

Discarded plastic has little value without a way to collect it, process it and make something useful. I developed PlastiVista to connect material recovery with Sol Seven Studios, a product design studio built around modular lighting.

Sort at the source.

I set up separate PLA and PETG collection bins at the RIT Fab Lab to recover failed prints and support material where they were being made. Keeping the plastics separate gave me a more controlled starting point for processing.

View the collection setup ↗

The shredder breaks the waste into flakes. I added a second grinding step to reduce particle size before feeding the extruder.

The shredder

The printer’s build volume set the enclosure strategy: more than 45 separate panels assembled into one housing. I made the access panels removable so the motor, cutting mechanism and controls could be adjusted during development.

Motor and controls

A 1 hp motor drives the cutting mechanism through a gear reducer. An Arduino Uno and Nextion touchscreen provide the operating interface, including jam clearing profiles.

The assembly includes a magnetic starter and a dedicated shutoff switch. Removable magnetic panels give access to the components.

The extruder

I needed a compact extruder I could build with available parts. I adapted an existing pellet extrusion design for shredded plastic, then assembled the drivetrain, heating system and controls.

Producing usable filament meant balancing three variables as the material moved through the machine.

Temperature
The heated barrel melts the plastic before it reaches the nozzle.
Auger speed
The rotating auger moves material through the barrel and controls the feed rate.
Pull speed
The motorized rollers draw out the filament. The pull rate has to match the extrusion rate to control its diameter.

The first
recycled print

On April 11, 2026, I completed the first successful print made entirely from the recovered filament: a small chair test. The material came from Fab Lab waste and discarded student prints.

Making the filament diameter consistent over longer runs is the next development goal.

Sol and Sol X

Sol Seven Studios is the product design side of this circular system. I developed a modular lighting line built around interchangeable bases, shades and dividers. A shared magnetic interface lets the same parts form different arrangements.

Sol X extends the idea with pogo pin contacts that pass power between modules from a single base.

View the assembly

The connection changed four times.

Enlarge image ↗

  1. Press fit

    Simple to assemble, but wear made the fit and alignment inconsistent.

  2. Slot and twist

    More controlled assembly, but alignment became more complex and configurations less flexible.

  3. Three magnets

    Easier to connect, but rotation still exposed problems with stability and alignment.

  4. Five magnets

    A more evenly distributed connection improved alignment and repeatability.

Then the constraint became power. Separate cords complicated stacked arrangements. Sol X adds pogo pin contacts so the modules share one powered base.

Next steps

The next phase is to develop standardized collection, sorting and processing for plastic waste from local businesses, validate material consistency and continue developing the lighting line. The larger ambition is local infrastructure that connects a reliable supply of recovered plastic with a product design studio, supporting an ongoing cycle of material recovery and new products.
